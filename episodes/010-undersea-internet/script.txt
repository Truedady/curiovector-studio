That message you sent overseas probably traveled underwater. Not through space.

Seriously.

When you open a video, it feels like the internet is floating invisibly around you.

Wi-Fi. Cell towers. Satellites.

So it's easy to imagine your data flying across the planet through the sky.

But zoom out.

Your phone sends a wireless signal to a router or cell tower.

And after that short hop, the journey becomes much more physical.

Your data races through networks of fiber-optic cables.

And if it needs to cross an ocean, it's likely to travel along the seafloor.

Inside those cables are incredibly thin strands of glass.

Equipment sends rapidly changing patterns of light through them, carrying digital information across enormous distances.

The surprising part? Much of an undersea cable isn't a giant steel pipe.

In deep water, it can be roughly as thick as a garden hose, with protective layers wrapped around tiny glass fibers.

And there are hundreds of these cable systems linking continents.

Why not just use satellites?

Satellites are vital for some places and services. But undersea fiber can carry extraordinary amounts of data at a much lower cost per bit.

Now here's the vulnerable part.

These cables can break.

Fishing equipment, ship anchors, and natural hazards can damage them.

When that happens, networks often reroute data through other connections. But places with few alternatives may suffer major outages.

So the internet isn't some weightless cloud.

It has a physical skeleton.

And a remarkable amount of our digital world depends on thin threads of glass resting on the ocean floor.
