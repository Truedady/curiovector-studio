Your phone can find you without internet. But the satellites aren't tracking you.

Imagine you're hiking somewhere remote. No cell service. No Wi-Fi. Yet your phone can still calculate where you are, even if the map itself won't load.

So how does it know?

You might imagine a satellite looking down, spotting your phone, and sending back your location.

But that's not how GPS works.

The satellites don't know you exist.

They simply broadcast their own position and the exact time.

And your phone listens.

Here's the clever part.

Those radio signals travel at the speed of light. By measuring how long each one took to arrive, your phone can estimate its distance from that satellite.

One distance isn't enough. You could be anywhere on an enormous sphere.

A second satellite narrows the possibilities.

A third narrows them further.

But your phone has a problem: its clock isn't nearly as accurate as the atomic clocks in space.

So signals from at least four satellites normally let the receiver solve two mysteries at once: where it is, and how far its own clock is off.

And there's one more twist.

Because those satellites move so fast, so high above Earth, even Einstein's theory of relativity matters. Engineers correct for its effects to keep GPS accurate.

Of course, GPS works best with a clear view of the sky.

But next time you see that tiny blue dot, remember:

No satellite had to find you.

Your phone found itself.

By listening to clocks in space.
