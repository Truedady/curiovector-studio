Ever heard your own voice in a recording and thought, Who is that?

Maybe it sounds thinner. Higher. Just... wrong.

You hear yourself talk every single day. So why does a simple phone recording make you sound like a stranger?

Here's the strange part.

When you speak, you're hearing your voice through two different paths at the same time.

The first is obvious.

Sound leaves your mouth, travels through the air, and reaches your ears.

That's roughly the path other people hear.

But there's a second route that nobody else gets.

Every time you speak, your vocal cords vibrate. Some of those vibrations travel through the tissues and bones of your head to your inner ears.

And that route changes the sound.

It adds a different mix of frequencies and physical vibrations, often making your own voice seem deeper or fuller to you.

Your brain grows up treating that combined sound as your normal voice.

Now press record.

A microphone mainly captures the sound traveling through the air.

When you play it back, your skull isn't carrying those same speech vibrations to your ears.

So one familiar part of your voice is suddenly missing.

That's why the recording can sound so strange, even when it's perfectly clear.

Of course, phone microphones and speakers can change the sound, too.

But the weird feeling isn't necessarily a bad recording.

It's a mismatch between the voice you've heard inside your head your entire life and the voice traveling through the room.

So next time you cringe at your recorded voice, remember:

The microphone didn't invent a stranger.

It let you hear something closer to what everyone else hears.
