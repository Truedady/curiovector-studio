An elevator cable snaps. What stops the car from falling?

The answer isn't a giant cushion at the bottom.

It's a hidden machine that's been protecting elevators for more than 170 years.

First, here's something most people don't realize.

A modern traction elevator usually hangs from several steel ropes, not just one. They're designed with safety margins, so one damaged rope doesn't automatically mean a free fall.

But what if something goes seriously wrong, and the car starts moving too fast?

That's where a second system takes over.

Hidden above the shaft is a device called an overspeed governor.

Think of it as a mechanical speed detector.

A separate rope moves with the elevator, spinning a wheel inside the governor.

At normal speeds, nothing happens.

But if the car accelerates past a dangerous limit, the governor trips and grabs that rope.

This activates safety brakes attached to the elevator car.

They grip the guide rails running along the shaft, bringing the car to a stop.

And the clever part? The emergency mechanism can work mechanically, without needing the building's electricity to tell it what to do.

There are other layers of protection, too, including machine brakes that engage when power is lost.

Now here's the part that sounds made up.

In 1854, an inventor named Elisha Otis stood on an elevated platform in front of a crowd and had its supporting rope cut.

The platform dropped just a little.

Then his safety mechanism caught it.

That demonstration helped make passenger elevators practical.

So the real secret of the skyscraper isn't just how we lift people hundreds of feet.

It's how we stop them safely.
